Chapter 7

Power Supply Unit

PSU types, wattage, cable management

Introduction

The Power Supply Unit (PSU) is the component that converts the alternating current (AC) from your wall outlet into the direct current (DC) voltages that every other component needs. It is easy to overlook, but a low-quality or underpowered PSU can cause crashes, instability, and even damage your hardware.

Choosing the right PSU involves more than just wattage. Efficiency ratings (80 PLUS), modular cabling, rail configuration, and build quality all matter. A good PSU can last through multiple system builds, making it a smart long-term investment.

In this chapter, you will learn how PSUs work, how to calculate wattage requirements, the difference between modular and non-modular units, and best practices for cable management.

How It Works

A PSU takes 110–240V AC input and converts it to multiple DC rails: +3.3V, +5V, and +12V. The +12V rail is the most important — it powers the CPU, GPU, and other high-draw components. The PSU uses a transformer, rectifier, and voltage regulators to produce clean, stable power. Efficiency measures how much of the input power is wasted as heat.

Household Object Analogy

Think of the PSU as the heart of your computer. Just as the heart pumps blood through your body, the PSU sends power through cables to every component. A weak heart cannot supply enough blood when you exercise — similarly, a weak PSU cannot supply enough power when your CPU and GPU are under load. The 80 PLUS rating is like a heart efficiency score — the higher it is, the less energy is wasted as heat.

PSU Types: Modular vs Non-Modular

PSUs come in three cable configurations:

Non-Modular

All cables are permanently attached. Cheaper but harder to manage — you must hide unused cables somewhere in the case.

Semi-Modular

Essential cables (24-pin, CPU power) are fixed. Extra cables like SATA and PCIe are detachable. A good balance of cost and convenience.

Fully Modular

Every cable is detachable. Only plug in the cables you need. Best for cable management and clean builds. Recommended for custom or small-form-factor PCs.

80 PLUS Efficiency Ratings

80 PLUS is a certification that guarantees a PSU is at least 80% efficient at various loads. Higher ratings mean less wasted energy and lower electricity bills:

80 PLUS White / Bronze

White: 80% at 20–100% load. Bronze: 82–85% efficiency. Entry-level but functional for budget builds with moderate power draw.

80 PLUS Silver / Gold

Silver: 85–88%. Gold: 87–90%. Gold is the most popular choice for gaming PCs — excellent efficiency at a reasonable price.

80 PLUS Platinum

89–92% efficiency. Used in high-end builds and servers. The reduced heat output can improve overall system cooling.

80 PLUS Titanium

90–94% efficiency. The highest certification. Near-zero power loss at 50% load. Reserved for flagship builds and data centres.

Calculating Wattage

To determine the wattage you need, add up the power draw of your components. The CPU and GPU are the biggest consumers. A mid-range build (e.g., Ryzen 5 + RTX 4060) needs about 500–600W. A high-end build (i9 + RTX 4090) needs 850–1000W or more. Always leave 20–30% headroom for spikes and future upgrades.

Cable Management Best Practices

Good cable management improves airflow, makes the build look professional, and simplifies future upgrades. Route cables behind the motherboard tray whenever possible. Use cable ties to bundle similar cables together. Connect the 24-pin motherboard cable and CPU power cable first, then route GPU and SATA cables. Keep cables away from fans and high-heat areas.

Key Insight

Never cheap out on the PSU. It is the one component that can destroy every other part if it fails. Buy a reputable brand with an 80 PLUS Bronze or higher rating. A good PSU can last 10+ years and outlive multiple system upgrades.

Vocabulary Table

Term Definition
PSUPower Supply Unit — the component that converts AC wall power into DC voltages required by computer components
WattageA measure of electrical power (watts) that a PSU can deliver to components. Higher wattage supports more powerful hardware
80 PLUSA certification programme that rates PSU efficiency at various load levels, from White (80%) to Titanium (94%)
ModularA PSU design where cables can be detached from the unit, allowing only needed cables to be connected
RailA voltage line within a PSU (usually +12V, +5V, +3.3V). Single-rail and multi-rail designs distribute power differently
Cable ManagementThe practice of routing and securing cables inside a computer case for improved airflow and aesthetics
SATA PowerA power connector from the PSU used for SATA storage drives such as HDDs and SSDs
EPS ConnectorAn 8-pin (or 4+4 pin) power connector that supplies power to the CPU from the PSU

Fun Facts

A high-end gaming PC under full load can draw as much power as a microwave oven — around 800 to 1000 watts.

The 80 PLUS Titanium rating requires a PSU to be at least 90% efficient at 10% load — meaning even at idle, very little power is wasted as heat.

Most PSU failures are caused by capacitor wear from heat. Higher efficiency PSUs run cooler and last significantly longer than inefficient ones.

Some high-end PSUs include a "zero RPM" fan mode — the fan does not spin at low loads, making the computer completely silent during light use.

Knowledge Check

1. What does the 80 PLUS rating measure in a PSU?

Answer: The efficiency of converting AC to DC power

2. Why is a fully modular PSU preferred for cable management?

Answer: You only plug in the cables you need, reducing clutter

3. Which voltage rail is most important for powering the CPU and GPU?

Answer: +12V
