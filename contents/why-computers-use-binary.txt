Why Do Computers Speak in 1s and 0s?If you ask a human to count, they’ll almost certainly use the numbers 0 through 9. This is the decimal system (or Base-10). It makes perfect sense for us—after all, we evolved with ten fingers, making Base-10 the most natural way for our ancestors to count and calculate.But if you look deep into the "brain" of any smartphone, laptop, or supercomputer, you won't find the numbers 2 through 9. You will only find 0s and 1s. This is the binary system (Base-2).So, why do computers use binary? If humans built computers to help us solve problems, why didn't we build them to think the way we do?The answer comes down to hardware, reliability, and the beautiful simplicity of logic.1. Computers Are Just Billions of Tiny SwitchesTo understand binary, you have to understand what a computer actually is at a physical level.At the heart of every computer processor are microscopic components called transistors. A transistor is a tiny piece of semiconducting material, usually silicon, that acts like an electronic valve. Unlike a regular light switch that you flip with your finger, a transistor uses a small electrical signal to control a much larger flow of electricity. It can either block the current or let it pass through.For the purpose of computing, this means a transistor really only has two functional states:Electricity is flowing (ON)Electricity is stopped (OFF)Because there are only two physical states, it is incredibly easy to assign a number to each state. We call "ON" a 1 and "OFF" a 0.When a computer is "thinking," it is rapidly flipping billions of these microscopic switches on and off. But they don't just act independently. Transistors are wired together so that the electrical output of one switch controls the input of the next one. This cascading domino effect—where electricity flowing through one path decides whether electricity can flow through another path—is the physical foundation of everything a computer does. Every video you watch, every text you send, and every video game you play is ultimately just a massive, complex arrangement of these "ON" and "OFF" states.2. Why Not Use Decimal in Hardware? (The "Dimmer Switch" Problem)"Okay," you might ask, "but why can't we build a switch with 10 states instead of just 2?"In theory, we could! In fact, early computer scientists experimented with decimal-based computers. To do this, you would use different levels of electricity (voltage) to represent different numbers. For example:0 volts = 01 volt = 12 volts = 2...and so on, up to 9 volts = 9.But here is the problem: the physical world is messy. Wires get hot, power supplies fluctuate, and electronic components create "noise" (interference).Imagine you have a traditional light switch. It’s either flipped up or down. Even if the switch gets a little wiggly, you still definitely know if the light is on or off. This is binary.Now imagine you have a sliding dimmer switch with ten exact, tiny notches marked 0 to 9. If you are trying to slide it quickly, in the dark, while someone is shaking the wall... you are probably going to accidentally push it to notch 4 when you meant to push it to notch 5.If a computer used ten different voltage levels, a tiny bit of electrical interference could make a 3.8-volt signal look like a 4.1-volt signal. The computer would mistake a '3' for a '4', leading to catastrophic calculation errors.By sticking to just two states—"High voltage" (1) and "Low voltage" (0)—the computer has massive margin for error. Even if the signal fluctuates wildly, the computer can still easily tell the difference between "mostly on" and "mostly off." This makes binary incredibly reliable.3. The Power of "True" and "False"Aside from hardware limitations, binary is also perfectly suited for logic.In the 1800s, a mathematician named George Boole invented a type of algebra based entirely on logic. In "Boolean algebra," everything is simply True or False. There are no "maybes."It turns out that you can solve incredibly complex math and logic problems using only True/False statements strung together. And conveniently, True and False map perfectly onto 1 and 0 (or ON and OFF).By wiring transistors together into microscopic configurations called "logic gates," engineers can create circuits that make actual decisions. For example, you can wire two transistors together so that current only flows out if both transistors are ON (an AND gate), or if at least one is ON (an OR gate).Because the rules of binary math are so simple (1 + 1 is just 10 in binary!), it is incredibly fast and cheap to build these physical circuits to process them. You chain enough of these simple True/False gates together, and suddenly you have a machine capable of rendering 3D graphics or calculating the orbit of a rocket.Summary: A Perfect CompromiseComputers don't use binary because it's the most efficient way to represent numbers—in fact, writing numbers out in binary takes up way more space (the number 200 in decimal is a long 11001000 in binary!).Computers use binary because it is the most reliable way to physically build a machine that does math. By reducing the universe of numbers down to just two states—ON and OFF, 1 and 0—we can build machines out of simple, error-proof switches. And by packing billions of those switches onto a tiny silicon chip, we can simulate the complexity of the entire world.
